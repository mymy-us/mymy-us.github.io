    How many bad parts does a Cpk of 1.33 really predict?

    By Tim Y |Posted on Jan 31, 2026 | 5 min read

    Topic: Translating process capability into business risk
    The academic view: Calculate $C_{pk}$. If it meets the required threshold, the process is capable.
    The workplace reality: Management does not need the index by itself. They need to know how many parts may be affected and what that could cost. But the translation is meaningful only when the statistical assumptions are true.

    You present a $C_{pk}$ of 1.33. The quality team understands it. Then a manager asks a much simpler question:

    “How many bad parts does that mean?”

    The familiar answer is about 63 defective parts per million. That answer can be useful, but it is not automatic.

    Why doesn’t management care about my Cpk number?

    $C_{pk}$ tells an engineer how close the process is to its nearest specification limit relative to the process variation. It does not directly tell management how many parts, how much sorting, or how much money is at risk.

    DPPM usually means defective parts per million. It gives the conversation a physical scale. A manager may not react to 1.33, but “about 66 affected parts per million for this characteristic” is easier to connect to production volume.

    The word “about” matters.

    So does a Cpk of 1.33 mean 63 defective parts per million?

    Only under a specific model. Before using the conversion table, check that:

    • The measurement system is adequate.
    • The process is statistically stable.
    • A normal distribution fits the data well enough.
    • The mean is centered between two specification limits.
    • The subgrouping and within-process variation used for $C_{pk}$ make sense.
    • You are discussing one measured characteristic, not every possible defect on the part.

    If one of these conditions fails, the table may still be a reference, but it is not a reliable prediction of shipped quality.

    Where does the number 63 come from?

    The distance from the process mean to the nearest specification limit is:

    Nearest specification distance in sigma = $3 \times C_{pk}$

    When $C_{pk}=1.3333$, the nearest specification limit is about four standard deviations from the mean.

    For a normal distribution, about 31.7 parts per million fall beyond one four-sigma limit. A centered process has the same tail on the other side:

    $31.7 + 31.7 = 63.4$ model PPM

    If the reported $C_{pk}$ is exactly 1.33, the distance is 3.99 sigma and the result is closer to 66 PPM. Many conversion charts report 63 because they treat 1.33 as the rounded form of 1.3333.

    This difference is small enough for a management estimate. It is still worth understanding so you do not present false precision.

    What table can I use?

    The following values use the printed $C_{pk}$ numbers exactly. They assume a stable, centered, normal process with two specification limits.

    Reported $C_{pk}$Nearest specification distanceCentered, two-sided model PPMPlain-language translation
    0.672.01 $\sigma$About 44,400About 4.4% nonconforming for this characteristic
    1.003.00 $\sigma$About 2,700About 0.27% nonconforming
    1.333.99 $\sigma$About 66About 0.0066% nonconforming
    1.675.01 $\sigma$About 0.54Less than one model PPM
    2.006.00 $\sigma$About 0.002About two per billion under the model

    Outside specification does not automatically mean scrap. A nonconforming part may be sorted, reworked, accepted under deviation, or scrapped. Those outcomes have different costs.

    A $C_{pk}$ of 1.33 is a common requirement, but it is not a universal law. The customer, characteristic, and production phase determine the actual acceptance threshold.

    Why can the same Cpk produce a different total PPM?

    $C_{pk}$ uses whichever specification limit is closer to the process mean. It tells you the nearest tail, but it does not fully describe the other side.

    If the process is perfectly centered, both tails are equal and you can double the one-sided probability. If the process is off-center, the far tail is smaller. If the characteristic has only one specification limit, there is only one tail to count.

    For an off-center or one-sided process, use $C_{pu}$, $C_{pl}$, or capability software instead of blindly doubling the nearest-tail result.

    Why are Cpk and Ppk telling me different stories?

    $C_{pk}$ normally uses within-subgroup variation. It describes the capability the process could achieve if shifts and drift between subgroups were removed.

    $P_{pk}$ uses overall variation. It includes more of what happens across time, so it is often closer to what the customer actually experiences.

    If $C_{pk}$ looks good but $P_{pk}$ is much lower, the process may be moving between shifts, batches, machines, tools, or days. Converting only the better $C_{pk}$ into PPM can make the risk look smaller than it is.

    When possible, report three views:

    • $C_{pk}$ for potential capability
    • $P_{pk}$ for overall performance
    • Observed PPM from the actual sample or production record

    If they disagree, investigate the difference instead of choosing the most comfortable number.

    Why does Six Sigma sometimes mean 0.002 and sometimes 3.4?

    The 0.002 PPM result comes from a centered, two-sided, six-sigma normal model with no assumed shift.

    The familiar Six Sigma value of 3.4 DPMO uses a conventional 1.5-sigma shift. It also uses DPMO, which means defects per million opportunities.

    DPMO is not the same as defective parts per million. One part can have several defect opportunities and can contain more than one defect. Do not place 0.002 PPM and 3.4 DPMO in the same table without explaining the different assumptions.

    And do not call either one perfection. Neither number is zero.

    What should I say in the meeting?

    Do not stop at:

    “Our $C_{pk}$ is 1.0.”

    Use a complete translation:

    “Our within-subgroup $C_{pk}$ is 1.0. Under a stable, normal, centered, two-sided model, that corresponds to about 2,700 nonconforming parts per million for this characteristic. Our overall $P_{pk}$ is [value], and our observed PPM is [value].”

    Then apply the production volume:

    Expected affected parts per week = weekly volume $\times$ model PPM $\div$ 1,000,000

    If management needs a cost estimate:

    Estimated direct exposure = expected affected parts $\times$ average cost per nonconforming part

    A part caught internally and a part that escapes do not carry the same cost, so calculate separate cases when necessary.

    $C_{pk}$ is not a financial prediction by itself. It is one input. State the assumptions, compare it with $P_{pk}$ and observed performance, then translate the result into parts and money.
