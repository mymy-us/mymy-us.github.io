    How does PPAP become your legal shield?

    By Tim Y |Posted on Dec 30, 2025 | 5 min read

    Topic: The true purpose of the Production Part Approval Process
    The academic view: PPAP demonstrates that a supplier can consistently meet the engineering requirements at the quoted production rate.
    The workplace reality: The approved PPAP package freezes your product’s technical baseline. When the customer changes their story later, that package gives you power to negotiate.

    Six months into mass production, the customer complains that the surface texture is too rough or the Rockwell hardness is too low. They want sorting, rework, and a corrective action.

    Your first instinct may be to defend the part or accept the blame. Do neither yet. Pull out the last approved PPAP package.

    What exactly did the customer approve?

    PPAP is much more than the Part Submission Warrant (PSW). Think of the PSW as the signature sitting on top of a complete technical package: drawings, design records, MSA and Gauge R&R studies, capability results, material and performance tests, PFMEA, Control Plan, samples, and other customer-required evidence.

    Once the PSW is approved, that package becomes the frozen baseline for production.

    “Frozen” does not mean the product can never change. It means this approved version remains the reference until a controlled change replaces it. If the customer requests an upgrade, the part number or engineering revision may change. The related drawings, tests, quality documents, and PPAP records follow that change. A new PSW approval then creates the next frozen baseline.

    With the baseline open in front of you, the complaint becomes less emotional.

    Which approved requirement did the part violate?

    That one question separates three very different situations:

    1. The part violates the approved drawing, specification, test result, Control Plan, or accepted sample. That is a supplier problem.
    2. The part matches the approved package, but the customer now wants something different. That is a new expectation or engineering change.
    3. The package never defined the disputed condition clearly. Both sides must negotiate the gap.

    The customer can request a change. They cannot reasonably use a new preference to declare previously conforming batches defective when the parts match the approved baseline and no other binding requirement was missed.

    Still, opening the meeting with “You approved it” usually creates another fight. A more tactful approach is to recognize the complaint, show the historical baseline, and offer a controlled path for improving future production without forcing either side to admit fault before the evidence is reviewed.

    The paperwork may be clear while the physical evidence is less perfect.

    What if the customer never signed my master sample?

    A formally customer-approved master sample is your strongest physical evidence. An internal sample signed only by your own production team does not carry the same weight.

    History still matters. If the sample has been retained for months or years, production has used it as the standard, and the customer has accepted repeated batches made to that condition, it becomes useful negotiation evidence. It does not override the drawing, but it shows that the disputed condition was part of the established product and had been accepted over time.

    You can say:

    “This condition is visible on our retained production sample and has also been present in previously accepted batches. We will review your requested improvement for future production, while we compare the current parts against the approved requirements.”

    That response protects your position while giving the customer a practical way to move the discussion forward.

    The frozen baseline only protects you while your factory continues to follow it.

    What happens when the process changes?

    Process improvement is allowed. Quiet process improvement is where trouble begins.

    A reduced cycle time, different cutting-tool brand, machine relocation, lower inspection frequency, or replacement of a core process operator may affect the approved process. Whether a change requires customer notification depends on the PPAP rules, customer-specific requirements, supplier agreement, and approved Control Plan.

    Before implementation, assess what the change could affect, update the related quality records, and submit whatever notification and evidence the customer requires.

    Sometimes the customer’s system issues automatic approval based on the type and risk of the change. Other changes need a technical review, testing, interim approval, or a complete PPAP resubmission. Notification is only enough when the applicable customer process says it is enough.

    As a junior engineer, do not guess. Ask your supervisor before promising anything or changing the process. Watch how experienced engineers judge the risk, read the contract, choose their wording, and manage the customer relationship. Ask why they made each move. Learning their reasoning is more useful than copying their sentences.

    Why is changing first and asking later so dangerous?

    Once the real production process no longer matches the approved package, you lose your strongest shield.

    If a quality crisis involves several suppliers and the true cause is difficult to prove, an unapproved change makes your company the easiest target. Even if the change improved the part, you broke the agreed baseline and damaged your credibility.

    The financial consequences usually come from the supplier eligibility agreement, purchase contract, or customer-specific terms. Those documents may define rework costs, sorting charges, warranty exposure, fines, suspension, or loss of future business. PPAP records show what changed; the contract determines what the violation can cost.

    An unapproved change may also create problems during an IATF 16949 audit because your change-control process and customer-specific compliance can be questioned. The outcome varies, but none of the useful outcomes are cheap.

    What should I do when a complaint arrives?

    Work in this order:

    1. Find the latest approved drawing, revision, PPAP package, and PSW status.
    2. Compare the current part and process against that frozen baseline.
    3. Decide whether the complaint is a true nonconformance, a new expectation, or an undefined gap.
    4. Review the master sample, accepted shipment history, engineering changes, and deviations.
    5. Check the supplier agreement for responsibility and penalties, then involve your supervisor before responding.

    Do not copy and paste PPAP documents just to get a signature. Treat the package like court testimony. When every record tells the same story, boring paperwork becomes negotiating power.
