    The part failed the specification. Can we still ship it?

    By Tim Y |Posted on Apr 1, 2026 | 7 min read

    Topic: Quality judgments and concessions
    The academic view: Specifications are black and white. Conforming parts ship, and nonconforming parts are rejected.
    The workplace reality: If you scrap every harmless deviation, some products will never make money. A senior quality engineer has to decide which risks the company can carry without destroying the customer relationship.

    An inspector puts a part on your desk. The glue bead is slightly higher than the drawing allows.

    The easy answer is “Scrap it.”

    The expensive answer is also “Scrap it.”

    Before making that call, put the mating part beside it. Where does the mating part stop? Does the extra glue enter that space? Does the assembly still fit and work?

    The glue height may fail the specification while the actual assembly still works perfectly. This is when you consider a concession.

    But first, you need to know whether the failed number predicts a failed product.

    Does failing the number mean the product will fail?

    No. It means the part failed a stated requirement. Whether that failure matters to the product is a separate engineering question.

    Start with Form, Fit, and Function:

    • Does the deviation change the part’s shape or appearance in a way the customer will notice?
    • Does it interfere with the mating part or assembly process?
    • Does it change how the product works?

    Form, Fit, and Function is the first filter. The team may also need to consider safety, durability, regulations, and the FMEA risk. A high RPN or an unusually severe failure mode gives you much less room to play.

    Cosmetic does not always mean harmless. A scratch on a hidden surface may not matter. The same scratch on the visible face of the final product may be the defect the customer cares about most, which is a function.

    Still, customers often write very stringent requirements at the beginning of a program because they hate defects and do not want to inherit supplier risk. Some limits are necessary. Some are more conservative than the real assembly needs.

    The quality engineer needs to find out what happens when the number is missed.

    Once the analysis proves that the extra glue does not interfere with the mating part, the technical question is mostly answered. The uncomfortable question remains: whose name goes on the release?

    The mating parts work. Can Quality release them alone?

    The structural analysis shows enough clearance. Your part is a little longer than nominal, but the mating part is a little shorter. You test the combination, and it fits without interference.

    The part is still nonconforming on paper. The evidence says the assembly can use it.

    This is where an internal concession may save the batch.

    For a truly minor issue, Quality may already have authority to document and release it. When the decision depends on structure, tolerance stack, assembly, durability, or customer reaction, Quality should not carry the gamble alone.

    Bring in the relevant departments. Engineering confirms the technical case. Production identifies the affected material. Program or management accepts the business exposure. Quality records the decision and controls the release.

    The signatures matter. If the decision later becomes a problem, the record shows that the company accepted the risk together. It was not one quality engineer quietly making parts disappear from the hold area.

    In practice, companies do not ask the customer about every microscopic deviation. They first ask whether the product still mates, works, and reaches the customer without a visible problem. If the evidence is strong and the risk is small, they may handle it internally.

    That is dirty manufacturing reality. It is also why the internal concession must be documented.

    The glue example can be handled inside the company when the evidence is strong and the risk is small enough. But what happens when the requirement belongs to the customer, or the company refuses to carry the risk alone?

    When does the customer enter the room?

    I saw the other side of concession while working on a roof ambient-light product for a German automotive customer.

    The specified material could not pass the melting test. The design allowed only that material. We could not satisfy both conditions at the same time.

    So we did not pretend the test had passed. We prepared a concession request and asked the customer to waive the test for that application.

    They approved it.

    The material did not suddenly gain a new physical property. The customer decided that accepting the failed test was better than forcing an impossible design combination.

    That is an external concession. The requirement owner sees the deviation, reviews the risk, and formally accepts it. This only works for a requirement the customer has authority to waive. Neither the supplier nor the customer can sign away a legal or regulatory obligation.

    The customer can also refuse. They may simply answer, “The PSW is signed and the PPAP is frozen. This is your problem.”

    A concession request asks the customer to take part of your risk. They do not owe you a yes.

    At least the melting test gave us a clear line: the material passed or failed. The next situation is harder. What if the inspector finds a strange condition that nobody defined?

    How can anyone concede a defect with no boundary?

    Sometimes the inspector finds a condition that is not shown in the SIP, boundary samples, or PPAP documents. Production has already made parts, and the operators have no comparison standard.

    Do not let every inspector invent a different answer.

    Contain the affected material, study how the condition affects Form, Fit, and Function, and find the worst condition the authorized team can accept. That physical part can become a temporary boundary sample.

    Identify it clearly. Record the part number, condition, applicable batch or period, date, and approvals. Put a controlled working sample at the inspection station so the operators compare against the same boundary.

    If the risk is more than minor, report it to your supervisor and share it with the relevant departments. If the condition affects function or carries a high FMEA risk, prepare for customer approval and prepare for rejection.

    The boundary sample does not rewrite the PPAP baseline. It gives the temporary gray area a visible edge.

    Now the inspectors have a line to compare against. Production immediately asks the next question: how much of the batch can we release?

    Does a healthy batch buy you more room?

    AQL sounds like the answer, but it is only a sampling rule used to decide whether a lot should be accepted or rejected. It is not a defect allowance that lets you knowingly hide bad parts inside the shipment.

    But the condition of the batch still changes how much risk people are willing to take.

    If the process is stable and the rest of the batch looks healthy, management may accept a few borderline cosmetic parts through the proper internal concession.

    If the process is unstable and defects are already pushing the lot toward rejection, that confidence is gone. The same borderline parts may be held, reworked, or scrapped to protect the shipment.

    The specification did not change. The company’s appetite for another escape did.

    That appetite is difficult to show because it is not written on the drawing. Some teams describe their internal evaluation strictness with a rough score out of 100. This is not a comprehensive product grade, customer acceptance score, or official formula. It only expresses how much gray area the company is willing to accept for a subjective borderline condition.

    That comfortable threshold can move with one phone call.

    Why would an internal threshold move from 90 to 95 overnight?

    Under normal conditions, the team may describe its internal threshold as 90. The customer is satisfied, the process looks stable, and the company is willing to carry a little gray-zone risk for this type of cosmetic judgment.

    Then the customer calls about a quality complaint.

    Tomorrow morning, the internal evaluation threshold becomes 95. The product itself did not receive a new score. The team simply became less willing to concede anything near the boundary.

    The drawing may be exactly the same, but the customer is now hyper-sensitive. They will inspect the next shipment with a magnifying glass, and management knows that another escape will cost much more than the scrap.

    A borderline mark that passed last month may be scrapped this month. Inspection becomes tighter. The factory may add over-inspection, extra approval, or a temporary internal guard band until trust returns.

    That five-point increase is the complaint tax.

    The glue part began as a failed number. Before release, it pulled in mating evidence, shared authority, batch confidence, and customer trust. The final check must make all of those pieces visible.

    What needs to be known before anyone signs?

    Before releasing anything, answer these questions:

    1. Which requirement did the part miss, and by how much?
    2. Does the actual part still mate, assemble, and function?
    3. What do the tolerance stack, test results, and FMEA say about the risk?
    4. Is this within Quality’s authority, or must other departments or the customer approve it?
    5. Which parts, batches, quantities, or dates does the decision cover?
    6. How will inspectors identify the boundary, and when does the approval expire?

    Making a concession is controlled risk management. Shipping the deviation without disclosure is cheating.

    A good concession shows the defect, proves why the product can still work, and puts the accepted risk in front of everyone whose signature belongs on it.
