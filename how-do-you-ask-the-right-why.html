    How Do You Ask the Right Why?

    By Tim Y |Posted on May 6, 2026 | 5 min read

    Topic: Root cause analysis and the 5 Whys
    The academic view: Ask “Why?” five times and eventually you will reach the root cause.
    The workplace reality: One badly aimed question can send the whole analysis in the wrong direction. You may fill every box and still end up blaming the operator or trying to redesign a machine that is working normally.

    The difficult part of 5 Whys is not finding five answers. It is knowing which question deserves to come next.

    Keep the initial Whys inside physics and process. Ask what physically changed. What moved, loosened, wore, cracked, overheated, or lost support. Then ask which setup, tool, material, or operating condition created that state.

    Do not begin with why an operator forgot, why management failed, or what solution you want to install. First prove how the failure became physically possible. Then trace that mechanism into the process that allowed it.

    Even a question about physics can follow the wrong branch.

    Can a physical Why still send you the wrong way?

    A bottle falls from a machine while it is running.

    You ask, “Why did it fall?” Someone answers, “Because the machine vibrated.” That sounds physical, so you ask why the machine vibrated. Soon you are discussing motors, bearings, and an expensive machine redesign.

    Someone else takes an easier route: “The operator did not place the bottle correctly.” A few Whys later, the answer becomes “carelessness,” followed by retraining.

    Both analyses chose a direction before defining what actually failed. Physics first does not mean chasing every physical event upstream. You need to target the physical behavior that the process should have controlled.

    Which physical failure are you actually trying to explain?

    First check whether the vibration was within its normal operating range. If it exceeded that range, “Why is the machine vibrating excessively?” is a valid branch. Depending on the risk, you may also ask whether the bottle needed better protection.

    If the vibration was normal, “the machine vibrates” is not a useful problem statement. A running machine will vibrate a little. Removing all vibration may be difficult, expensive, and unnecessary.

    Try this instead:

    The bottle fell while the machine was operating within its normal vibration range.

    Now the first Why changes:

    Why could the bottle fall under a level of vibration it should normally tolerate?

    That question keeps the investigation on the failed relationship between the bottle and the process. It directs you toward stability, support, retention, and positioning before you wander into psychology or attempt to rebuild the machine.

    The normal-or-abnormal check keeps you from attacking an input the process was supposed to handle. It also keeps you from ignoring an abnormal condition merely because fixing it would be difficult.

    Which physical explanation should you follow?

    Perhaps the guide clearance was too large. Perhaps the bottle had insufficient support, too little friction, an unstable center of gravity, or an incorrect position. Resonance may also be involved.

    These are hypotheses, not answers.

    Look at the machine and the bottle. Check contact marks, settings, and wear points. Measure what you can. If practical, reproduce the failure under controlled conditions.

    Do not choose a cause only because it sounds clever or leads to an easy countermeasure. Continue the 5 Whys from the explanation that the evidence supports.

    Once you confirm the physical mechanism, the questions can move upstream.

    When should you leave physics and enter the process?

    Suppose the evidence confirms that the bottle fell because the guide gap was excessive. One possible analysis might continue like this:

    1. Why did the bottle fall under normal vibration? The guide gap allowed it to move beyond a stable position.
    2. Why was the guide gap excessive? The guide was set outside the required position.
    3. Why could it be set outside that position? The setup had no defined reference or physical stop.
    4. Why could production start in that condition? The setup check did not verify the guide gap.

    This is only an illustration. Every answer would still need evidence from the actual process.

    The first questions establish how the failure happened. The later questions explain why the process created or allowed that physical condition.

    You may reach a useful cause in four questions. You may need seven. Five is a reminder to keep digging, not a quota.

    But what happens when a person really did miss a step?

    Can operator error or management ever be a cause?

    Yes, but “operator error” is usually an event description, not a complete root cause.

    If an operator skipped a required step, record that fact. Then ask why the process could continue after the step was missed. Was the condition visible? Did the fixture prevent the next operation? Did the check occur at the right point? Was the expected method practical under the actual cycle time?

    The same applies to “management problem.” The label is useless. A specific failure, such as a missing maintenance trigger or design-review requirement, can be investigated and corrected.

    Do not ban people or management from the analysis. Ban vague answers that give you nothing to change.

    Why did nobody catch it?

    If the defect reached the next process or the customer, run a second 5 Whys track:

    1. Occurrence: Why did the process create the defect?
    2. Escape: Why did the controls fail to prevent or detect it before it moved forward?

    Fixing occurrence reduces the chance of making another bad part. Fixing escape reduces the chance of sending one away if occurrence control fails again. Do not force both tracks into one tangled chain.

    How do you know when to stop asking Why?

    Before closing the analysis, check four things:

    • Each answer is supported by observation, records, measurement, or testing.
    • You considered other reasonable causes and ruled them out.
    • The proposed action breaks the cause-and-effect chain instead of asking someone to “be more careful.”
    • You can verify afterward that the action prevented recurrence.

    If your preferred solution appeared before the cause was proven, step back. You may have written the 5 Whys backward to justify something you already wanted to do.

    Start with the unwanted physical behavior under the actual operating condition. Decide whether the input was normal or abnormal. Test the reasonable physics branches, then follow the confirmed mechanism into the process and system that allowed it.

    The quality of a 5 Whys analysis depends less on how many times you ask Why than on whether each Why keeps you on the right causal path.
