    Why does your Lean improvement disappear after you leave?

    By Tim Y |Posted on Dec 22, 2025 | 5 min read

    Topic: The reality of a Lean Engineer’s role
    The academic view: A Lean Engineer’s job is continuous improvement: find waste, fix it, and move to the next problem.
    The workplace reality: Your job has two halves: improving the process and making sure the improvement survives after you leave.

    Picture this: You analyze a bottleneck, test a solution with your own hands, and teach the line leader and operators. The process improves. You feel like a genius.

    Then you leave the shop floor.

    Soon, everyone is using the old method again. Your solution worked, but it could not survive without you.

    Isn’t solving the problem the whole job?

    Ask a professor what a Lean Engineer does, and you will probably hear about continuous improvement. Find waste. Remove it. Repeat.

    The corporate job contains two battles:

    1. The attack: Continuously eliminate waste.
    2. The defense: Keep the improvement alive.

    The attack creates a result. The defense turns that result into the normal way of working. Ignore the second half, and you become the permanent babysitter of your own project.

    This is where an introverted engineer, especially one who is new to the company, can fool themselves.

    Can I win the attack by myself?

    Sometimes, yes.

    You can run the data, identify a bottleneck, test the process, and design a fix alone. Some technical problems do not need a committee. Working alone can also feel safer when you are new. You avoid political arguments, control the analysis, and move at your own speed.

    You are a maverick. A hero in your head.

    That approach can produce a technically sound proposal. The trouble begins when other people must run it on every shift without you standing beside them.

    Why does everyone return to the old way?

    The operator’s first priority is keeping production moving by any means available. Getting parts out today comes first. Whether the method is fastest overall, produces the best quality, or protects your projected savings may not matter to them unless those results affect how their shift is judged.

    If the old method is familiar and your new method adds steps, depends on memory, or slows them down during a bad shift, guess which one they will use.

    This is not mysterious resistance. The operator is responding to the job as it has been structured. Your improvement is competing with today’s production pressure.

    Some attack work can be done alone. Before finalizing the change, however, let the line leader and operators try it under actual production pressure. They will find weaknesses that never appeared while the engineer was carefully running the test.

    Most new engineers then reach for documentation.

    Aren’t updated documents enough?

    You update the FMEA, Control Plan, Standard Operating Procedure, inspection instructions, parameter checksheet, and Product Lifecycle Management system. The paperwork is complete.

    That paper shield is necessary, but weak.

    Documents describe what should happen. They do not make the new method easier, catch someone drifting from it, or force anyone to react. Many operators will not read lengthy documents unless someone makes them. Punishment may create compliance while management is watching, but it also teaches people to hide deviations.

    A living improvement needs an active defense.

    What actually keeps an improvement alive?

    1. Name the process owner. Someone in production must own the new method after the Lean Engineer leaves.
    2. Involve the line leader and operators before release. Make them test the change, not merely attend the training.
    3. Make the new way obvious and easy to follow. Use tape, shadow boards, point-of-use instructions, or error-proofing where they fit.
    4. Verify the behavior. Layered Process Audits and Gemba walks show whether the method survives normal production.
    5. React to drift. When people return to the old way, find out whether the cause is training, tooling, workload, or a bad standard.

    These controls make the defense visible. They still require help from people you probably do not manage.

    What if nobody reports to me?

    Welcome to the corporate turf war.

    A Lean Engineer often asks another department to change while having no formal authority over its people. You cannot order them to listen, and saying “because it is Lean” will not make them care.

    Find the department head who owns the people and the process. Show what the change does for that department, such as reducing overtime, scrap, or line stoppages. Then ask the department head to endorse the change and assign someone to own it.

    When the department head says, “We are doing this,” the team follows. Let the manager use their authority. You remain the architect, provide the evidence, and keep checking whether the structure holds.

    That conversation can be uncomfortable when you are new. Sitting quietly in the back of the meeting is much easier.

    How confident should a new engineer sound?

    Walk into the project with this attitude:

    “I am the subject matter expert. Nobody knows this better than me.”

    Yes, that sounds arrogant. In corporate life, you sometimes need that level of confidence. If you present your own project like an apology, nobody will risk their reputation or resources to support it.

    Treat the statement as your posture, not a literal claim that you know everything. You own the analysis, the evidence, and the follow-through. The operator knows the daily work. Maintenance knows the equipment. The department head owns the people and resources.

    Do not hide in the corner or wait for the production manager to speak first. Frame the problem, show the evidence, state what help you need, and actively seek support from your own department.

    Confidence gives the project a spine. Listening prevents stupid decisions.

    So what are the two halves of a Lean job?

    The attack finds waste and proves that a better method can work. The defense creates the ownership and controls that keep people using it.

    Phase 1 leans heavily on analysis. Phase 2 leans heavily on system design, influence, and guts. Both still require technical judgment and people skills.

    A clever engineer can improve the process while standing there. A Lean Engineer can leave and find the improvement still alive.
