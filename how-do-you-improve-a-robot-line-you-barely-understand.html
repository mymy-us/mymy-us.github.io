    How Do You Improve a Robot Line You Barely Understand?

    By Tim Y |Posted on May 8, 2026 | 6 min read

    Topic: Lean improvement on automated production lines
    The academic view: Measure OEE, apply TPM, eliminate the losses, and maintain the gains.
    The workplace reality: The controls engineer is talking about CAN bus communication, the mechanical engineer is talking about servo torque, and the operator has already reset the fault before you understood what stopped. You start wondering what a Lean engineer can possibly add.

    You do not need to know every wire, program, and gearbox. You need to understand what the line should do, where it loses output, and who owns each kind of loss.

    That is what the TPM house can help you organize.

    Read the house from the bottom up. The 5S foundation makes abnormal conditions visible. The pillars divide the work needed to remove and prevent losses. No single engineer holds up the roof alone.

    As a junior Lean engineer, you can enter through the foundation and work upward.

    So where do I enter the TPM house?

    Start with 5S, but do not start by painting lines around everything.

    Follow one part from the first station to the last. Learn what each station changes, where the part waits, which checks allow it to continue, and what a normal cycle looks and sounds like. Then watch a fault recovery. Which station stopped first? What alarm appeared? What happened before the operator pressed reset?

    Now make the normal condition easier to see. A clean machine exposes fresh oil, loose bolts, damaged cables, metal shavings, and unusual wear. Organized tools and accessible inspection points make checks repeatable. On automated equipment, cleaning is also inspection.

    This foundation has a safety boundary. Observe from a safe position while the machine operates. Clean, inspect, lubricate, or clear jams only under the site’s approved safe condition, with guarding or energy isolation where required.

    A clean machine will not stay inspectable by itself. Someone must own the daily condition.

    Which pillar gives the operator a role?

    That is Autonomous Maintenance.

    The pillar does not mean handing the maintenance department’s work to operators. It gives operators safe, defined basic-care tasks that help them recognize an abnormal condition early.

    An operator may clean an approved area, inspect a known wear point, check a gauge against an approved range, or tag a problem for escalation. Give the operator time, tools, training, and a visual standard. Show what normal looks like, what abnormal looks like, what the operator may correct, and when maintenance must take over.

    Mark gauge limits only after engineering confirms them. Complex diagnosis, repairs, program changes, and work involving hazardous energy remain with qualified specialists.

    Autonomous Maintenance helps reveal abnormalities. It does not tell you which one deserves an improvement project. For that, move to another pillar.

    Which pillar tells me what to improve?

    Focused Improvement turns repeated interruptions into a specific problem for a team to solve.

    Begin with line output, not the loudest alarm. A station can be:

    • Faulted: It cannot complete its own work.
    • Starved: The previous station did not deliver a part.
    • Blocked: The next station cannot accept its finished part.

    The station with the red light may only be waiting for the real problem somewhere else. Find the constraint, meaning the process currently limiting the line’s output. Improving a fast robot that remains blocked will not give you more finished product.

    At the constraint, OEE separates the losses into three views:

    • Availability: The process was supposed to run but stopped.
    • Performance: It ran slowly or suffered minor stops.
    • Quality: It produced scrap or rework instead of good parts.

    The percentage alone cannot tell you what to fix. Record the station, time, machine state, fault code, product, duration, and recovery action. Compare the log with what operators and technicians saw. Automatic data may group several faults under one code or miss a quick manual reset.

    A micro-stop may last only long enough for an operator to clear a jam and press reset. One event looks harmless. A repeated pattern consumes production time, but nobody remembers every reset at the end of the shift. Focused Improvement makes that pattern visible and attacks the repeat cause.

    Changeovers can also become a focused-improvement project. On an automated line, SMED may include staged tooling and material, recipe selection, parameter verification, sensor adjustment, and first-piece approval. Move safe preparation outside the stopped period without bypassing safety or change control.

    Now you have a verified loss. You still need people who understand the machine.

    How do I lead experts who know more than I do?

    Bring the team a problem they can work with:

    • Where and when the loss occurs
    • The product, machine state, and fault code involved
    • The sequence immediately before the interruption
    • How often it repeats and how much production it affects
    • What temporarily restores operation

    The mechanical engineer can investigate the mechanism. The controls engineer can investigate logic, communication, or sensing. Maintenance can evaluate equipment condition and the maintenance plan. You keep the group focused on the verified loss, coordinate the actions, and check whether the result holds.

    The technical fix is only part of TPM. The improvement must enter the pillar that will prevent the loss from returning.

    Then what are the other pillars doing?

    The other pillars are not decorations, and a junior engineer should not simply ignore them. Treat the TPM house as a routing map:

    What the investigation revealsLikely TPM route
    A worn component or recurring breakdownPlanned Maintenance
    A machine creates or passes a defectQuality Maintenance and jidoka
    People cannot recognize or recover from the conditionEducation and Training
    The equipment was difficult to inspect, change, or maintain from the beginningEarly Equipment Management
    Spare parts, work orders, or purchasing delay the responseAdministrative and Office Maintenance
    Cleaning, inspection, or recovery exposes people to dangerSafety, Health, and Environment

    A loss may involve several pillars. The table helps you find the next owner; it is not a department chart. Safety also sets the boundary for every pillar rather than waiting at the end of the investigation.

    You may not own the budget, PLC program, maintenance plan, or equipment design. You still need to route the verified loss to the person who does. Otherwise the problem falls into the gap between departments and returns later.

    So how do you know whether the whole house is working better?

    What keeps the improvement standing?

    The countermeasure must become part of daily control. Depending on the cause, that may mean an Autonomous Maintenance check, a Planned Maintenance trigger, a better fault message, an error-proofing device, a standard recovery sequence, or feedback for the next equipment design.

    On an automated line, jidoka also matters. The equipment should distinguish normal from abnormal, stop safely when necessary, and make the reason visible instead of quietly producing defects.

    After the change, return to the line. Did good output improve? Did the interruption stop recurring? Did quality remain protected? Did the change create a new safety or workload problem?

    If the same reset stops appearing and good output improves, the TPM house did its job. The PLC can remain in the controls engineer’s hands.
