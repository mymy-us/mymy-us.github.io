    Why does Amoeba Management turn coworkers into rival businesses?

    By Tim Y |Posted on Feb 8, 2026 | 5 min read

    Topic: Amoeba-style internal profit centers
    The academic view: Give each department its own profit and loss statement. People will see the cost of waste, take ownership, and improve faster.
    The workplace reality: When department profit affects bonuses, coworkers begin acting like suppliers and customers. Every defect, favor, and hour of support needs an owner because somebody’s payout is at risk.

    Rejected pallets are sitting outside Assembly while sorting waits to start. Everyone agrees that the parts must be contained, but nobody agrees on who should pay.

    Machining says the damage happened after delivery. Assembly refuses the batch. Finance will not release the sorting order without a cost center. The quality meeting slowly turns into an argument between department managers.

    The parts have not moved, but the blame already has.

    This is the factory version of Amoeba Management discussed in this article: small units have their own P&L, buy and sell internally, and feel the result through their bonuses. Kyocera’s original model used the same small-unit accounting logic without directly paying unit profit as a team bonus. In daily factory life, the bonus-linked version is where the fight becomes much more visible.

    Why is nobody signing the sorting bill?

    From the company’s view, charging the loss to Machining or Assembly changes nothing. The same company pays the sorting company, the overtime, and the scrap.

    From each manager’s view, the cost code matters a lot. Put the bill on their P&L and their monthly result drops. If bonuses depend on that result, the argument becomes personal.

    This is why managers can spend more energy fighting over a charge than fixing the failure that created it. The accounting system has made cost allocation part of the crisis.

    Why does money make Kaizen move faster?

    There is a good side to this system.

    In a normal cost center, your team might save $100,000 through an improvement. The money disappears into the company account. The team receives a thank-you email and perhaps a pizza party.

    In a bonus-linked profit center, that saving remains visible on the department’s P&L. The scrap report drops. Overtime falls. An unnecessary outside service disappears. When bonus time arrives, the team can see why the improvement mattered.

    Waste is no longer an abstract Lean concept. It is taking money away from the department. People start hunting for it without waiting for another motivational speech.

    When did the next process become a real customer?

    Lean says, “The next process is your customer.” Amoeba-style accounting makes that relationship feel real.

    Machining sells parts internally to Assembly. When Assembly finds a defective batch, it can reject the transfer under company rules. The revenue may be reversed, and the sorting or rework cost may return to Machining.

    The producing department can no longer protect its numbers by pushing questionable parts downstream. A red-tagged pallet now hurts its financial result.

    That pressure can strengthen accountability. It does not replace the control plan, containment, traceability, inspection, or stop rules. Money gives people a reason to care about the defect. Engineering controls keep the defect from escaping when somebody does not care.

    Why does the transfer price become the real battleground?

    Machining wants a high internal selling price. Assembly wants a low purchasing price. Both managers can improve their own profit by winning that argument without changing one second of cycle time or removing one defect.

    Then a shared failure happens. Machining contributed to it. Assembly contributed to it. Perhaps Quality missed the warning. Each manager opens a spreadsheet and explains why another department should carry the loss.

    The dispute climbs to the general manager because nobody wants the cost sitting in their year-end result.

    Transfer prices and shared-failure rules need to exist before the crisis. If management invents the rules after the money is already lost, every decision looks like favoritism.

    Why would a team hide a good improvement?

    One department finds a better way to run a machine. Sharing it would help the entire company, but it could erase the team’s advantage in the internal ranking. If that ranking affects bonuses, keeping quiet can feel financially sensible.

    The best method stays inside one department, so the rest of the company never receives the benefit.

    Experience-sharing meetings help, but they do not remove the incentive. The company must recognize the team that created the improvement and share the reward when other departments adopt it. Otherwise, management asks people to give away the advantage it told them to protect.

    Can the accounting reward the wrong behavior?

    Consider Maintenance. If its internal revenue comes from repair hours, more breakdown work can make its P&L look better. Production needs fewer breakdowns. The two departments are now rewarded for opposite outcomes.

    Quality can fall into the same trap if it receives credit for inspection volume instead of preventing defects.

    Not every department needs to be a profit center. Some support teams work better as cost or service centers measured by response, reliability, quality, and spending. A fake internal market can create very real bad behavior.

    How do you stop the war without killing the motivation?

    Keep the local P&L, but put company-level limits around it:

    • Set transfer prices and shared-failure rules before disputes happen.
    • Separate controllable costs from costs the department cannot control.
    • Measure company flow, quality, delivery, and safety alongside department profit.
    • Share rewards when several departments create or adopt one improvement.
    • Give managers enough authority to control the numbers assigned to them.

    The department should feel the cost of its decisions. It should not be rewarded for moving the cost, delay, or risk to somebody else.

    What should a Lean engineer do inside this system?

    Learn exactly how your boss is measured. Find the department’s profit formula, bonus rules, transfer prices, and cost-allocation rules.

    Then bring two calculations to every proposal:

    1. What happens to this department’s P&L?
    2. What happens to the company’s total cost, flow, quality, and delivery?

    Do not walk into the meeting with nothing except “everyone should cooperate.” Show managers how the proposal affects the number they are responsible for. Then show why the company still wins.

    If the department wins while the company loses, you did not create a Lean improvement. You only moved the bill to another desk.
